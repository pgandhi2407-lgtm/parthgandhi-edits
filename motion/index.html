Editing with AI · free guide

Motion graphics on the laptop you already have.

By Parth Gandhi, video editor

I used to turn down heavy motion work because my laptop couldn't handle After Effects. Now I don't open it for that at all. The animation gets written as code and rendered somewhere else, so my laptop never does the heavy lifting.

The tool isn't the hard part. Knowing what good motion looks like is. This guide has both: the setup I use, the brief I copy-paste, and five rules for motion that doesn't look cheap.

The setup

  1. Describe the animation.In plain words, to Claude: what moves, where, and when. No keyframes, no timeline.
  2. Show a reference.Attach a short clip of a move you like. This does more than any adjective.
  3. It gets built in code.Claude writes the animation in Remotion, a code library for making videos, and renders the video. The render runs in the cloud, not on my laptop.
  4. Review it like a client would.I watch the render and leave notes on exact timecodes ("at 0:12, hold this longer"). Then it gets fixed and rendered again.

The brief that works

Copy it, fill in the brackets, attach your reference.
Animate [the element] in my [1080×1920 reel].

Style: [background], [font], [accent colour].
Reference: attached clip. Copy the motion, not the look.

What moves: only [the one thing that should move].
Timing: starts on the word "[word]", lands in about [0.4]s, holds until "[word]".
Ease: fast start, slow settle. No bounce, no overshoot.

Avoid: spinning, popping in, shaking, colour flashes, more than one effect at a time.
Keep all text inside the Instagram safe zone: x 100–940, y 250–1500.
Leave a short beat of stillness before and after the move.

What good looks like

Left is the version AI usually gives you first. Right is what to ask for.
01

Ease everything. Nothing moves at one speed.

Real things speed up and slow down. A move that starts fast and settles slowly feels placed; a linear one feels like a slideshow.

Ask for: "fast start, slow settle, no bounce"
02

Things arrive. They don't perform.

Spinning, popping and bouncing is the first idea every tool reaches for, which is exactly why it looks cheap. Let elements rise a little, come into focus, and stop.

Ask for: "rise 30px with a slight blur, then settle"
03

One effect at a time.

If the line rises in, that's the move. Save emphasis for the one word that matters. Stack five effects and nothing gets noticed.

Ask for: "word-by-word reveal, red box on '[word]' only"
04

Leave a beat. Let it breathe.

AI times things to the exact frame, so the next line slams in the moment the last one ends. Give each line time to land, hold, and clear before the next one comes.

Ask for: "hold 0.3s after it lands, clear, short gap, then the next line"
05

Stay inside the safe zone.

Instagram puts the caption at the bottom and the buttons on the right. Anything near the edges gets covered. Keep text centred, and use the top and bottom for space, not the sides.

Ask for: "keep text inside x 100–940, y 250–1500"

Collecting references

  1. Save the move, not the video.When a reel makes you stop, screen-record just the 2 seconds of motion you liked. That clip is your reference.
  2. Name it by what moves."cursor-draws-box.mp4" beats "ref_14.mp4". When you brief, you'll find it in seconds.
  3. One reference per animation.Three references for one move give you an average of all three. Pick the one that's closest.

One last thing

You don't need a better laptop. You need to know what good looks like. The rules above are the shortcut.
